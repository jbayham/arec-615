This document elaborates on the Model Labs described in the course syllabus.

## Overview

Model Labs are short, focused investigations of applied economic optimization models. Selected Thursday meetings begin with a paper and a shared effort to reconstruct its model: the economic decision, decision variables, objective function, constraints, inputs, solution concepts, and important assumptions. The Model Lab challenge then asks you to investigate one substantive question before the following Tuesday.

The point is not to write a polished research paper or to demonstrate that work was completed without AI. AI can help you code, derive, debug, interpret, and brainstorm. Your responsibility is to understand, verify, and explain the resulting model and evidence.

## A Model Lab Investigation

Choose a focused question that helps you learn something about the model. Depending on the lab, you might:

- reproduce a simplified model or an important numerical result;
- perturb a parameter, constraint, or objective coefficient and interpret the comparative statics;
- break the model by locating a surprising, implausible, or problematic assumption or parameterization;
- extend the model with one economically meaningful feature;
- reformulate the economic problem and compare the results; or
- explain a feature of the model with a derivation, visualization, or numerical experiment.

Early labs will be more structured and may include a working implementation. As the semester progresses, labs may instead begin with a partial implementation, data, or the original paper and leave more of the formulation and investigation to you.

## What to Submit

Keep the written deliverable concise. Include enough material for someone to understand the question, experiment, result, and interpretation:

- a concise statement of the question;
- the relevant model modification or computational experiment;
- essential equations or code;
- one central result, such as a figure, table, numerical solution, or solver output;
- a short economic and mathematical interpretation; and
- one substantive AI-generated claim, calculation, interpretation, derivation, or piece of code that you independently checked or validated.

You may also identify an unresolved question that emerged from the exercise. A lengthy report, separate repository, and polished slide deck are not required unless specified for a particular lab.

## Tuesday Discussion and Presentations

Every student must come prepared to explain the investigation. At the beginning of designated Tuesday meetings, approximately two or three students will be chosen at random to present. A presentation should answer:

1. What did you want to understand?
2. What did you change, build, solve, or test?
3. What did you learn, and why did the result occur?

Show the formulation and evidence needed for the class to follow the experiment, but prioritize explanation over presentation design. Be ready to reason from the model in response to questions about binding constraints, comparative statics, multipliers, uniqueness, and assumptions.

Students who are not selected to present will still participate as respondents. A respondent may be asked to predict another change, explain an implication, identify an assumption, connect the result to an optimization concept, or suggest a useful next experiment.

## Using AI Responsibly

Use AI as a computational and intellectual tool, not as a substitute for judgment. Check results independently, keep track of important assumptions, and be prepared to defend every claim you present. Do not enter confidential, restricted, proprietary, or identifiable data into public AI systems.

## Questions?

Please reach out during office hours or via email (before end of day Monday) if you would like feedback on a Model Lab question or investigation.
